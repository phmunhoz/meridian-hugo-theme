## Why this matters

Most teams that have outgrown their current platform already know something is wrong. The symptoms are familiar: every change requires an agency, time-to-market is measured in weeks, and the annual cost keeps creeping up with no proportional increase in value.

What's less clear is *when* the pain is severe enough to justify the disruption of a migration — and what that disruption actually looks like when done well.

## The real cost of staying

The mistake most teams make when evaluating a migration is looking only at the license fee. The full picture includes infrastructure, agency dependency for day-to-day changes, custom integrations that became their own maintenance burden, and the opportunity cost of slow time-to-market.

## When migration makes sense

Not everyone should migrate. There are scenarios where staying makes sense — operations with very specific customizations that the target platform genuinely can't replicate, or companies with large internal technical teams that extract real value from platform flexibility.

But for most mid-market operations, migration becomes the right call when:

- Maintenance costs exceed the value delivered by the platform
- The business team can't operate without depending on IT for simple changes
- Time-to-market is blocking commercial initiatives
- Required integrations exist natively in the target ecosystem

## What changes after go-live

The most immediate change — and the one that surprises clients most — is autonomy. Marketing and operations teams that previously needed to file a ticket for any change start doing it themselves: editing content, creating promotions, configuring shipping.

This has an important secondary effect: the IT team stops being a bottleneck and can focus on integrations and projects that actually require development.
